# Understanding Distributed Consensus: Raft, Paxos, and ZooKeeper (ZAB)

Distributed consensus is one of the most fundamental problems in distributed systems: how do a group of nodes agree on a single value even when some nodes fail or the network is unreliable? Over the years, several algorithms have emerged, with **Paxos**, **Raft**, and **ZooKeeper’s **ZAB** (ZooKeeper Atomic Broadcast)** becoming the most influential. In this post, I’ll take you through the inner workings of each, compare their strengths and weaknesses, and explain where they shine in practice.

---

## 1. Paxos – The Theoretical Cornerstone

Paxos, developed by Leslie Lamport in the late 1980s (though published later), is the original consensus algorithm. It’s renowned for its correctness proof but infamous for its difficulty to understand and implement.

### How Paxos Works

Paxos defines three roles:

- **Proposer**: Suggests a value.
- **Acceptor**: Votes on proposals.
- **Learner**: Learns the final agreed value.

In its basic form (*classic Paxos*), a single round of consensus (agreement on one value) goes through two phases:

1. **Prepare Phase**  
   A proposer chooses a unique round number `n` (higher than any it has seen) and sends a `Prepare(n)` request to a majority of acceptors.  
   Acceptors reply with a promise: they will never accept any proposal with a number less than `n`, and they return the highest-numbered proposal they have already accepted (if any).

2. **Accept Phase**  
   If the proposer receives responses from a majority, it selects the value associated with the highest round number among the responses (or its own value if none). Then it sends an `Accept(n, value)` request to the same acceptors.  
   An acceptor accepts the proposal unless it has already promised to ignore rounds lower than `n`. Once a majority of acceptors accept, the value is *chosen*.

### Multi‑Paxos – Making It Practical

Classic Paxos agrees on a single value, but real systems need to replicate a stream of operations. *Multi‑Paxos* introduces a stable leader to avoid repeating the Prepare phase for every operation. The leader, once elected, can skip the Prepare phase and just run the Accept phase for subsequent entries, achieving high throughput.

### Strengths

- **Proven Correctness**: Paxos’s mathematical proof is rock‑solid.
- **Flexibility**: It allows arbitrary roles and can be adapted to many scenarios.
- **No Leader Dependency**: The basic version works without a dedicated leader, though Multi‑Paxos uses one.

### Weaknesses

- **Complexity**: The algorithm is notoriously hard to implement correctly. Subtle bugs often arise in production.
- **Lack of Practical Details**: The original description omits many details needed for a working system (e.g., leader election, reconfiguration).
- **Performance Under Leader Failure**: Recovery can be involved, especially in classic Paxos.

### Where It’s Used

- **Google Chubby** – a lock service that uses Paxos internally.
- Many internal distributed databases (e.g., some NoSQL systems) use Paxos or its variants under the hood.
- **FoundationDB** – originally used a Paxos variant.

Despite its complexity, Paxos remains the gold standard for correctness.

## 2. Raft – Designed for Understandability

Raft was created in 2013 by Diego Ongaro and John Ousterhout at Stanford with a clear goal: be understandable enough that developers could implement it without pulling their hair out. It achieves this by decomposing consensus into three independent subproblems.

### How Raft Works

Raft nodes are always in one of three states: **Leader**, **Follower**, or **Candidate**. Time is divided into *terms* (epochs), each beginning with an election.

#### 2.1 Leader Election
- Followers are passive; they only respond to requests from leaders and candidates.
- If a follower doesn’t hear from a leader within an *election timeout*, it becomes a candidate, increments its term, and requests votes.
- A candidate wins if it receives votes from a majority of nodes. The new leader then sends periodic heartbeats to maintain authority.
- To avoid split votes, Raft uses random election timeouts.

#### 2.2 Log Replication
- The leader accepts client requests, appends them to its local log as *log entries*, and sends `AppendEntries` RPCs to all followers.
- A follower appends the entry if its previous log entry matches the leader’s (ensuring consistency).
- Once a majority have replicated the entry, the leader *commits* it and applies it to the state machine. Followers eventually commit as well.

#### 2.3 Safety
Raft enforces several rules to guarantee safety:
- **Election restriction**: A candidate can only win if its log is at least as up‑to‑date as the voter’s. This ensures that the leader always has all committed entries.
- **Log matching**: If two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, they are identical up through that index.
- **Leader append‑only**: Leaders never overwrite or delete entries in their own log.

### Strengths

- **Understandability**: The decomposition into elections, replication, and safety makes Raft far easier to implement than Paxos.
- **Strong Safety Guarantees**: Well‑defined rules prevent anomalies.
- **Active Ecosystem**: Many open‑source libraries (e.g., etcd’s Raft) are battle‑tested.

### Weaknesses

- **Leader Bottleneck**: All writes go through the leader, which can become a throughput limitation.
- **Performance During Leader Failure**: Recovery can take seconds (election timeout), causing a service interruption.
- **Less Flexible**: The protocol is more rigid compared to Paxos.

### Where It’s Used

- **etcd** – Kubernetes’ backing store.
- **TiKV** – a distributed key‑value store.
- **CockroachDB** – a geo‑distributed SQL database.
- **MongoDB**, **MariaDB**, and many other databases now embed Raft for replication.

Raft has become the go‑to consensus algorithm for new distributed systems due to its simplicity and robustness.

## 3. ZooKeeper’s ZAB – Atomic Broadcast for Coordination

ZooKeeper is a centralized coordination service widely used for distributed locks, configuration management, and service discovery. Its heart is the **ZooKeeper Atomic Broadcast (ZAB)** protocol, which is often compared to both Paxos and Raft but has distinct characteristics.

### How ZAB Works

ZAB defines two main phases:

- **Leader Election**: Similar to Raft’s election, but with a key difference: ZAB elects a leader based on the **zxid** (ZooKeeper Transaction ID) – a 64‑bit number combining an epoch and a counter. The node with the highest zxid wins, ensuring the new leader has the most up‑to‑date state.
- **Atomic Broadcast (Recovery + Broadcast)** :  
  - After election, the leader synchronizes with followers to bring their logs up to date. This *recovery phase* ensures all followers have the same data before normal operation begins.
  - In normal operation, the leader broadcasts proposals (transactions) to followers. Each proposal is assigned a unique zxid. Followers send acknowledgements, and when a majority acknowledge, the leader commits the proposal and notifies followers.

ZAB guarantees **total order** of all transactions: if a transaction is delivered to one server, it is delivered to all servers in the same order. This ordering is crucial for coordinating tasks like leader election, locks, and configuration changes.

### Differences from Raft

While both are leader‑based and use majority votes, ZAB and Raft differ in important ways:

| Aspect | ZAB (ZooKeeper) | Raft |
|--------|-----------------|------|
| **Ordering** | Total order of *transactions*; guarantees causal order across clients. | State machine replication: logs are replicated; commit index provides consistency. |
| **Recovery** | Requires a full synchronization phase after leader election. | Leader can start serving immediately after election; followers catch up via normal AppendEntries. |
| **Client‑Server Interaction** | Clients can read from any node (eventually consistent reads) or perform sync for strong consistency. | Usually all writes go through leader; reads can be from followers for performance. |
| **Failure Handling** | Leader failure triggers a recovery phase that may block writes for a few seconds. | Similar, but recovery may be faster due to fewer phases. |

### Strengths

- **Strong Ordering Guarantees**: The total order of transactions simplifies building higher‑level coordination primitives.
- **Mature Ecosystem**: ZooKeeper is battle‑tested in thousands of production deployments (Kafka, HBase, etc.).
- **Hierarchical Namespace**: The file‑system‑like API makes it easy to implement distributed data structures.

### Weaknesses

- **Performance**: Under high write loads, ZooKeeper can become a bottleneck because all writes go through the leader.
- **Write‑Heavy Limitations**: It is not designed for large data storage; it’s a coordination service, not a database.
- **Leader Recovery**: The recovery phase can be expensive, especially if the leader fails with a large log.

### Where It’s Used

- **Apache Kafka** – uses ZooKeeper for controller election and metadata management (though Kafka is moving to Raft‑based KRaft).
- **Apache HBase**, **Apache Solr**, **Apache Spark** – rely on ZooKeeper for coordination.
- **Service discovery** in many microservices architectures.

ZooKeeper remains the de facto standard for distributed coordination where strong ordering and a battle‑tested implementation are required.

## 5. Choosing the Right Algorithm

There’s no one‑size‑fits‑all solution. Your choice depends on your goals:

- **If you need a proven, highly flexible foundation** and have the expertise to implement it correctly, **Paxos** (usually Multi‑Paxos) is the gold standard. Most engineers today, however, prefer to avoid implementing Paxos directly and instead rely on a library or a higher‑level system.

- **If you are building a new distributed database or a system that requires a clear, easy‑to‑implement consensus layer**, **Raft** is the obvious choice. Its clean separation of concerns and extensive open‑source implementations (like etcd/raft) make it a safe bet.

- **If you need a battle‑tested coordination service with strong ordering guarantees** and are willing to accept the operational overhead of ZooKeeper, **ZAB** through ZooKeeper is still an excellent option. Many large‑scale systems (e.g., Kafka, HBase) rely on it.

In recent years, Raft has gained massive momentum because of its simplicity and the success of projects like etcd and TiKV. However, Paxos remains deeply embedded in many infrastructure systems, and ZooKeeper continues to power a huge number of distributed applications.

## Conclusion

Distributed consensus is a fascinating area where theory meets practice. Paxos laid the mathematical foundation, Raft made it accessible, and ZAB brought reliable coordination to countless systems. Understanding the trade‑offs between these algorithms helps you make informed decisions when designing or operating distributed systems.

Whether you’re building the next scalable database or just curious about how systems like Kubernetes stay consistent, I hope this deep dive helps you appreciate the beauty and complexity behind the consensus protocols that keep our distributed world running.
